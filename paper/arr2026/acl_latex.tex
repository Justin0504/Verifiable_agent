\begin{abstract}
Fact attribution verifiers --- models that judge whether a claim is supported by a source document --- are critical for trustworthy LLM deployment.
Existing verifiers produce opaque binary labels that reveal nothing about \emph{which} evidence was considered or \emph{why} the judgment was made, and are trained with reward signals that collapse all verification quality into a single bit.

We introduce \seva, a structured evidence verification framework that produces multi-granularity output --- evidence alignment spans, step-by-step reasoning chains, calibrated confidence, and error diagnosis from a six-category taxonomy.
Training models on such multi-component output poses a reward design challenge: we show that binary reward \emph{structurally fails} for GRPO on structured output, producing near-zero advantage spread.
Our \emph{process reward} function addresses this by scoring five verification components independently, with process-oriented components (alignment + chain) receiving 60\% of total weight.
This creates a smooth optimization landscape where GRPO first masters verification \emph{behavior} (alignment: $0.917 \to 0.997$, chain: $0.917 \to 0.995$), then improves label accuracy --- an implicit curriculum without explicit scheduling.

Because structured output makes failure modes transparent, \seva further improves through a self-evolution loop (\textsc{Verify}$\to$\textsc{Reflect}$\to$\textsc{Probe}$\to$\textsc{Refine}) that generates adversarial data targeting the model's specific weaknesses.
On ClearFacts, \seva-3B with GRPO achieves +4.1 F1 over SFT; scaling to 7B with two-stage fine-tuning yields 68.6 F1, surpassing zero-shot Llama-3.1-8B while providing interpretable verification output.
We release our code, reward function, and structured verification dataset.\footnote{Code and data: \url{https://github.com/Justin0504/Verifiable_agent}}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ==============================================================

Fact attribution verification --- determining whether a claim is supported by a source document --- underpins the reliability of retrieval-augmented generation \citep{gao2023rarr}, hallucination detection \citep{min2023factscore}, and factuality-based alignment \citep{tian2024finetuning}.
As LLMs are deployed in increasingly high-stakes settings, verifiers serve as the last line of defense against unfaithful generation.
Yet recent audits reveal that even state-of-the-art verifiers suffer from systematic errors \citep{seo2025vtv}, raising a fundamental question: \emph{can we build verifiers that not only classify, but explain and improve?}

Current verifiers such as MiniCheck \citep{tang2024minicheck} and ClearCheck \citep{seo2025vtv} produce binary labels --- ``Attributable'' or ``Not Attributable'' --- without revealing which evidence was considered or what reasoning led to the judgment.
Consider a claim stating ``60\% of participants significantly improved'' against a source saying ``60\% of subjects showed improvement.''
A binary verifier outputs ``Not Attributable,'' but this tells us nothing about \emph{where} the mismatch lies: is it the percentage, the population descriptor, or the qualifier ``significantly''?
Without such transparency, debugging and downstream correction become guesswork.

A natural remedy is to train verifiers to produce \emph{structured} output --- evidence alignment spans, step-by-step reasoning chains, and error diagnoses.
This raises a new challenge: how should such models be trained beyond supervised fine-tuning?
Reinforcement learning with outcome-based (binary) reward has proven effective for mathematical reasoning \citep{shao2024deepseekmath, zha2025tango}, where a model must produce a single correct answer.
But structured verification output is fundamentally different: the model must simultaneously produce valid JSON, grounded evidence spans, coherent reasoning chains, \emph{and} the correct label.
In early training, the model rarely gets all components right at once, so binary reward is near-uniformly zero and GRPO receives no learning signal.
We find this is not merely ``less effective'' --- \textbf{binary reward fails entirely for structured output RL}, a negative result that generalizes beyond verification to any task requiring multi-component generation.

To address this, we propose a \emph{process reward function} that decomposes verification quality into five independently scored components --- format, evidence alignment, reasoning chain, label accuracy, and error diagnosis.
Process-oriented components (alignment + chain) receive 60\% of total weight, while the label contributes only 15\%.
This creates a smooth optimization landscape where a response with correct evidence analysis but wrong label receives ${\sim}0.60$ reward (vs.\ $0.0$ under binary reward).
An important consequence emerges during training: GRPO first learns to produce high-quality structured output (alignment: $0.917 \to 0.997$, chain: $0.917 \to 0.995$), and only then improves label accuracy --- the process reward induces an \emph{implicit curriculum} from verification behavior to verification outcome, without any explicit scheduling.

Structured output also unlocks a capability unavailable to binary verifiers: \emph{targeted self-improvement}.
Because the model produces explicit evidence alignments and error diagnoses, failure analysis reveals not just \emph{that} the model errs, but \emph{why} --- which error types are systematically missed, where evidence grounding breaks down.
This diagnostic signal drives a self-evolution loop (\textsc{Verify}$\to$\textsc{Reflect}$\to$\textsc{Probe}$\to$\textsc{Refine}) that generates adversarial examples targeting the model's specific weaknesses, enabling continuous improvement without additional human annotation.

We instantiate these ideas in \seva (Self-Evolving Verification Agent; Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}), making three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item A \textbf{structured verification schema} with evidence alignment, reasoning chains, calibrated confidence, and a six-category error taxonomy (\S\ref{sec:format}), paired with a \textbf{process reward function} that enables RL training where binary reward structurally fails (\S\ref{sec:reward}).
  \item A \textbf{self-evolution loop} that leverages structured output as diagnostic signal, generating targeted adversarial data to iteratively strengthen the verifier (\S\ref{sec:evolution}).
  \item Empirically, GRPO with process reward improves \seva-3B by +4.1 F1 over SFT with near-perfect structural quality.
  Scaling to 7B with two-stage fine-tuning yields 68.6 F1, surpassing zero-shot Llama-3.1-8B while providing interpretable, auditable verification output (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ==============================================================

\paragraph{Fact attribution verification.}
The task of determining whether a claim is supported by a source has roots in NLI \citep{bowman2015snli}, textual entailment \citep{dagan2005pascal}, and fact verification \citep{thorne2018fever}.
Recent work shifts focus to \emph{attribution} in LLM-generated text: MiniCheck \citep{tang2024minicheck} trains a 7B verifier via NLI transfer, AlignScore \citep{zha2023alignscore} proposes unified alignment scoring, and ClearCheck \citep{seo2025vtv} constructs cleaner benchmarks with synthetic multi-hop augmentation.
LLM-AggreFact \citep{tang2024llmaggrefact} provides a unified evaluation across 11 benchmarks.
A common thread: all produce binary labels and all use one-shot SFT, leaving both interpretability and training signal on the table.

\paragraph{RL for verification and generation.}
GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath} enables RL training without a learned critic, and has been applied to mathematical verification \citep{zha2025tango} and multi-agent RAG generation \citep{li2026march}.
Dr.Zero \citep{chen2025drzero} introduces boundary-optimal weighting for proposer-solver co-evolution.
These works demonstrate that RL can improve verification quality, but all operate in settings where the output is a single answer or binary label --- the reward design challenge for \emph{structured} multi-component output remains unexplored.

\paragraph{Process reward models.}
PRMs for mathematical reasoning \citep{lightman2024lets, wang2024math} score intermediate steps independently of final answers, providing denser training signal than outcome-only reward.
Our process reward draws on this intuition but differs in a key way: instead of scoring reasoning \emph{steps} sequentially (where step $k$ depends on step $k{-}1$), we score verification \emph{components} (alignment, chain, diagnosis) that contribute independently.
This decomposition is what enables the smooth reward landscape that makes GRPO viable for structured output.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% ==============================================================

Given a claim $c$ and a source document $d$, our goal is to train a verification model $\pi_\theta$ that produces \emph{structured} output --- evidence alignment, step-by-step reasoning, calibrated confidence, and error diagnosis --- rather than an opaque binary label.
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} provides an overview.
We decompose our approach into four components:
(1)~a \textbf{structured verification schema} that defines the multi-granularity output format (\S\ref{sec:format}; Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}, top right);
(2)~a \textbf{process reward function} that independently scores each output component, creating a smooth optimization landscape (\S\ref{sec:reward}; Figure~\ref{fig:reward_detail});
(3)~a \textbf{three-phase training pipeline} --- SFT data generation, supervised fine-tuning, and GRPO with process reward (\S\ref{sec:training}; Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline});
and (4)~a \textbf{self-evolution loop} (\textsc{Verify}$\to$\textsc{Reflect}$\to$\textsc{Probe}$\to$\textsc{Refine}) that iteratively strengthens the model against its own weaknesses (\S\ref{sec:evolution}; Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}, bottom).

\subsection{Structured Verification Schema}
\label{sec:format}

\seva produces a structured output $\mathbf{v} = (A, C, y, \gamma, e, s)$ with four complementary components:

\paragraph{Evidence alignment ($A$).}
A list of $(c_i, d_i, \text{status}_i)$ triples mapping claim spans to source spans, where each \texttt{status} $\in$ \{\texttt{match}, \texttt{mismatch}, \texttt{not\_found}\}.
This forces the model to ground its verification in specific textual evidence, making the decision auditable.

\paragraph{Reasoning chain ($C$).}
Step-by-step verification, where each step examines a \texttt{claim\_part} against \texttt{source\_evidence} with a \texttt{judgment} $\in$ \{\texttt{supported}, \texttt{not\_supported}, \texttt{partially\_supported}\} and a natural language \texttt{explanation}.

\paragraph{Label and confidence.}
Binary label $y \in \{\text{Attributable}, \text{Not Attributable}\}$ with calibrated confidence $\gamma \in [0,1]$.

\paragraph{Error diagnosis.}
When $y = \text{Not Attributable}$, the output includes an error type $e$ from a six-category taxonomy (Table~\ref{tab:error_taxonomy}) and a natural language fix suggestion $s$.
This provides actionable feedback for downstream correction.

This multi-component schema is expressive, but training models to reliably produce it introduces a new challenge: what reward signal can guide RL when the output has so many moving parts?

\subsection{Process Reward Function}
\label{sec:reward}

Binary reward --- 1 if the label is correct, 0 otherwise --- is the default choice for RL-based verification.
Binary reward (1 if the label is correct, 0 otherwise) collapses all verification quality into a single bit, providing no signal for \emph{how} well the model reasons.
We instead decompose the reward into five independently scored components:
\begin{align}
R = \; & \underbrace{w_f R_f + w_a R_a + w_c R_c}_{\text{process (70\%)}} + \underbrace{w_l R_l + w_d R_d}_{\text{outcome (30\%)}} + R_{\text{cal}}
\label{eq:reward_full}
\end{align}
where $R_f$ (format), $R_a$ (alignment), and $R_c$ (chain) reward the verification \emph{process}, while $R_l$ (label) and $R_d$ (diagnosis) reward the \emph{outcome}.
Weights are $w_f{=}0.10$, $w_a{=}w_c{=}0.30$, $w_l{=}w_d{=}0.15$.
An additional calibration term $R_{\text{cal}}$ rewards well-calibrated confidence and penalizes overconfident wrong answers.
Full scoring rubrics are in Appendix~\ref{app:reward_detail}.

\paragraph{Design rationale.}
Process components (alignment + chain) dominate at 60\% of total weight, while the label contributes only 15\%.
This ensures that a response with correct evidence analysis but wrong label ($R \approx 0.60$) scores far higher than a correct label with no reasoning ($R \approx 0.28$), as shown in Table~\ref{tab:reward_landscape}.
This smooth landscape is critical: under binary reward, GRPO receives near-zero advantage spread when the model rarely predicts correctly, causing training to stagnate (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Training Pipeline}
\label{sec:training}

With the reward landscape in place, we turn to the question of how to progressively build verification capability (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

\paragraph{Phase 1: SFT data generation.}
We generate structured annotations for ANLI \citep{nie2020adversarial} training samples using GPT-4o-mini as teacher.
Each $(c, d, y)$ triple is annotated with the full structured schema (\S\ref{sec:format}).
Annotations are validated for format correctness and filtered for quality, yielding 4,992 structured training samples (Appendix~\ref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Phase 2: Supervised fine-tuning.}
We fine-tune Qwen2.5-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} on the structured annotations.
For 3B, we use full fine-tuning (3 epochs, lr=$2\times10^{-5}$).
For 7B, we adopt a two-stage approach: (a)~\emph{general SFT} on 59K binary-format samples from six datasets (ANLI, ClearFacts, FEVER, HoVer, VitaminC, SciFact) to build broad verification capability; then (b)~\emph{structured SFT} on 5K SEVA-format samples to learn the multi-component output.
For 7B models, we use LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} targeting all attention and MLP projections.
Full hyperparameters are in Appendix~\ref{app:impl}.

\paragraph{Phase 3: GRPO with process reward.}
We apply GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath} with the process reward (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_full}).
For each prompt, $G{=}8$ responses are sampled at temperature 1.2 with top-$p{=}0.95$.
We apply boundary-optimal weighting \citep{chen2025drzero}: groups where approximately half the responses score above median receive higher weight, focusing gradient on the decision boundary.
Training runs for 350 steps using veRL \citep{shao2024verl} with FSDP on 2$\times$RTX 6000 Ada, lr=$2\times10^{-6}$, KL coefficient $\beta{=}0.001$.

\subsection{Self-Evolution Loop}
\label{sec:evolution}

The SFT$\to$GRPO pipeline yields a capable verifier, but a binary verifier trained the same way would stop here.
Structured output opens a different path: because the model produces explicit evidence alignments and error diagnoses, we can inspect its failures at a granularity that binary labels never afford.
We exploit this diagnostic signal through an iterative self-evolution loop (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}, bottom).
Each round proceeds in four stages:

\paragraph{Verify.}
Evaluate the current model on the training distribution and held-out benchmarks, collecting all failure cases with their structured outputs.

\paragraph{Reflect.}
Analyze failure patterns: which claim types cause the most errors, which error categories are under-detected, and where evidence alignment breaks down. This produces a \emph{weakness profile} identifying the model's blind spots.

\paragraph{Probe.}
Generate targeted adversarial examples that exploit the identified weaknesses using six perturbation strategies (entity confusion, numerical perturbation, multi-hop grafting, paraphrase, presupposition injection, unanswerable wrapping; Appendix~\ref{app:adversarial}).
Each strategy targets a specific failure mode --- e.g., if the weakness profile shows poor entity disambiguation, the adversarial generator prioritizes entity substitution examples.

\paragraph{Refine.}
Retrain the model on the original data augmented with the new adversarial examples, using GRPO with the process reward.
This creates a virtuous cycle: better structured output enables more precise weakness identification, which produces more effective adversarial data, which in turn improves structured output quality.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% ==============================================================

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
We evaluate on ClearFacts \citep{seo2025vtv}, a cleaned fact attribution benchmark (1,590 samples) where ambiguous and mislabeled examples have been corrected.
We additionally evaluate on three diverse verification benchmarks: FEVER \citep{thorne2018fever}, TruthfulQA \citep{lin2022truthfulqa}, and HaluEval \citep{li2023halueval} (200 stratified samples each).
These benchmarks span encyclopedic claims (FEVER), common misconceptions (TruthfulQA), and LLM-generated hallucinations (HaluEval), testing generalization across domains.
Dataset statistics are in Appendix~\ref{app:data}.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
\textit{Binary-label:} from VtV \citep{seo2025vtv}: MiniCheck-7B \citep{tang2024minicheck} (81.2 F1), ClearCheck-8B ($\sim$84 F1), Llama-3.1-8B zero-shot (67.2 F1), Claude 3.7-Sonnet (86.0 F1), o1 (85.4 F1), R1-Qwen2.5-32B (82.9 F1). We additionally evaluate MiniCheck-Flan-T5-Large (770M).
\textit{Structured:} GPT-4o-mini with zero-shot SEVA prompting, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with zero-shot chain-of-thought SEVA prompting.
\textit{Ours:} \seva-SFT and \seva-GRPO at 3B; \seva-SFT at 7B with LoRA (rank 64 and 128).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Standard: accuracy, macro F1.
Structural: evidence alignment quality ($R_a$), chain quality ($R_c$), groundedness (fraction of spans found in source text), chain consistency (agreement between step judgments and final label).
Calibration: Expected Calibration Error (ECE).

Table~\ref{tab:multi} compares \seva against external baselines across four benchmarks.
GPT-4o-mini achieves the highest FEVER F1 (91.0) but struggles on TruthfulQA (48.6) and HaluEval (34.0), where its structured outputs show high confidence on incorrect predictions.

However, GRPO shifts the model toward predicting ``Not Attributable'' more aggressively (false positive rate of 35.9\% on ClearFacts; Appendix~\ref{app:error}), which hurts positively-skewed benchmarks like HaluEval.
We hypothesize that the asymmetric reward structure --- where the diagnosis component ($R_d$) provides additional signal only for ``Not Attributable'' predictions --- creates an incentive for negative predictions.
Addressing this through label-balanced reward calibration is a clear direction for future work (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ==============================================================

\paragraph{The implicit curriculum effect.}
Our experiments reveal an unexpected training dynamic: GRPO with process reward does not improve all components uniformly.
Instead, structural quality saturates first (alignment: $0.917 \to 0.997$, format: $72\% \to 100\%$), while label accuracy improves more gradually ($64.9 \to 69.0$ F1).
The model learns \emph{how to verify} before it learns \emph{what to predict} --- without any explicit curriculum scheduling.
This echoes findings from mathematical PRMs \citep{lightman2024lets}, where rewarding intermediate steps implicitly teaches problem decomposition, but extends the principle to a fundamentally different output structure.

\paragraph{The reward asymmetry problem.}
Our error analysis reveals that GRPO shifts the model toward predicting ``Not Attributable'' more aggressively (35.9\% false positive rate).
We trace this to the reward function itself: the diagnosis component $R_d$ provides richer signal for negative predictions (error type + fix suggestion) than for positive ones (correct omission = 1.0).
This creates an asymmetric incentive that binary reward would not produce.
The finding highlights a general tension in process reward design: richer output schemas for certain classes can inadvertently bias the model toward those classes.
Label-conditional reward calibration is a clear direction for future work.

\paragraph{Scale vs.\ RL: partial substitutability.}
An intriguing empirical pattern: 7B-SFT with LoRA-128 (68.5 F1) nearly matches 3B-GRPO (69.0 F1).
Model scale and RL training appear to be partially substitutable for structured verification --- a larger model can reach through SFT what a smaller model needs RL to achieve.
This suggests that combining both (7B + GRPO + self-evolution) should push beyond either axis alone.
The remaining gap with MiniCheck-7B (81.2 F1) stems largely from training data scale (5K structured vs.\ 57K binary) and LoRA capacity (2--4\% vs.\ 100\% trainable parameters), both addressable.

\paragraph{Structured output as a diagnostic interface.}
Beyond interpretability for end users, structured output serves a second role that we believe is underappreciated: it provides a \emph{diagnostic interface} for self-improvement.
Binary verifiers can only tell you their accuracy; \seva's evidence alignments and error diagnoses reveal \emph{which} failure types persist, enabling the Probe stage to generate adversarial data with surgical precision.
This is qualitatively different from generic data augmentation and suggests a broader principle: structured output may be valuable not just for transparency, but as a first-class signal for iterative training.

% ==============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ==============================================================

We set out to build fact attribution verifiers that explain their reasoning, improve from their mistakes, and can be trained with RL despite producing complex structured output.
\seva addresses all three goals.
Its process reward function resolves a structural incompatibility between binary reward and multi-component output, enabling GRPO to first master verification behavior (alignment: $0.997$, chain: $0.995$) and then improve classification accuracy (+4.1 F1 over SFT).
Its structured output serves double duty: providing interpretable verification for end users, and acting as a diagnostic signal for self-evolution.

These findings point to a broader principle: for tasks requiring structured generation --- whether verification, code analysis, or clinical reasoning --- the reward function must decompose quality at the granularity of the output, and the output itself can drive targeted self-improvement.
We release our code, process reward implementation, and structured verification dataset.

% Limitations

\section*{Limitations}
Our evaluation covers ClearFacts and five auxiliary benchmarks (200 samples each); broader evaluation on LLM-AggreFact \citep{tang2024llmaggrefact} would strengthen generalization claims.
The structured training data (5K samples) is an order of magnitude smaller than datasets used by binary baselines like MiniCheck (57K); scaling annotation is a straightforward path to improvement.
Our 7B experiments use LoRA (2--4\% trainable parameters); full fine-tuning with GRPO is expected to substantially close the gap with larger binary verifiers.
Self-evolution results beyond Round~0 are in progress and will test whether the implicit curriculum effect compounds over multiple rounds.
The reward asymmetry identified in \S\ref{sec:discussion} (35.9\% false positive rate) indicates that label-balanced reward calibration is needed before deployment.
Finally, structured output increases inference cost; investigating distillation or structured pruning to reduce this overhead is future work.

\paragraph{Boundary-optimal weighting.}
Following \citet{chen2025drzero}, we apply a group-level scaling factor:
\begin{equation}
\small
s = 0.5 + 0.5 \times (1.0 - |p_{\text{correct}} - 0.5| \times 2.0)
\end{equation}
where $p_{\text{correct}}$ is the fraction of responses in a group scoring above the group median.
Groups near the decision boundary ($p_{\text{correct}} \approx 0.5$) receive full weight ($s{=}1.0$), while groups that are trivially easy or hard ($p_{\text{correct}} \approx 0$ or $1$) receive reduced weight ($s{=}0.5$).

\section{Process Reward: Detailed Scoring Rubrics}
\label{app:reward}
\label{app:reward_detail}

This section provides the complete scoring rubrics for each reward component, corresponding to the implementation in \texttt{seva\_reward.py}.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:process_reward} summarizes the full computation procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\small
\caption{Process Reward Computation}
\label{alg:process_reward}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Response $r$, gold label $y^*$
\ENSURE Reward $R \in [-0.10, 1.28]$
\STATE Parse $r$ as JSON $\to$ $\hat{v}$
\IF{parse fails}
  \RETURN $R = 0.0$
\ENDIF
\STATE $R_f \gets \text{ScoreFormat}(\hat{v})$ \COMMENT{0/0.2/0.5/1.0}
\STATE $R_a \gets \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a \in A} \text{ScoreAlign}(a)$
\STATE $R_c \gets \frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{s \in C} \text{ScoreStep}(s) + \text{LenBonus}$
\STATE $R_l \gets \mathbf{1}[\text{normalize}(\hat{y}) = y^*]$
\STATE $R_d \gets \text{ScoreDiagnosis}(\hat{e}, \hat{s}, y^*)$
\STATE $R \gets 0.10 R_f + 0.30 R_a + 0.30 R_c + 0.15 R_l + 0.15 R_d$
\IF{$\hat{y} = y^*$}
  \STATE $R \gets R + \hat{\gamma} \times 0.15$ \COMMENT{calibration bonus}
\ELSE
  \STATE $R \gets R - \hat{\gamma} \times 0.10$ \COMMENT{overconfidence penalty}
\ENDIF
\RETURN $R$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\label{tab:aliases}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Alias} & \textbf{Canonical label} \\
\midrule

\subsection{$R_a$: Alignment Scoring Detail}

For each alignment entry $a_i$, the per-entry score is:

\begin{equation}
\small
\begin{aligned}
R_a(a_i) = & \; 0.3 \cdot \mathbf{1}[|\texttt{claim\_span}| > 0] \\
          + & \; 0.3 \cdot \mathbf{1}[|\texttt{source\_span}| > 0 \;\lor\; \texttt{NOT\_FOUND}] \\
          + & \; 0.2 \cdot \mathbf{1}[\texttt{status} \in \text{VALID}] \\
          + & \; 0.1 \cdot \mathbf{1}[3 \leq |\texttt{claim\_span}| \leq 200] \\
          + & \; 0.1 \cdot \mathbf{1}[3 \leq |\texttt{source\_span}| \leq 500]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The final alignment score is the mean across entries, capped at 1.0.

\subsection{$R_c$: Chain Scoring Detail}

For each reasoning step $s_j$:

\begin{equation}
\small
\begin{aligned}
R_c(s_j) = & \; 0.3 \cdot \mathbf{1}[\texttt{judgment} \in \text{VALID}] \\
          + & \; 0.3 \cdot \mathbf{1}[|\texttt{explanation}| \geq 10] \\
          + & \; 0.2 \cdot \mathbf{1}[|\texttt{source\_evidence}| \geq 5] \\
          + & \; 0.2 \cdot \mathbf{1}[|\texttt{claim\_part}| > 0]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

A length bonus $\min(|C|/3, 1) \times 0.2$ rewards multi-step reasoning chains over single-step explanations.

\subsection{$R_d$: Diagnosis Scoring Detail}

\begin{equation}
\small
R_d {=}
\begin{cases}
1.0 & y^* {=} \text{A}, \text{no err.} \\
0.3 & y^* {=} \text{A}, \text{err.\ present} \\
0.6{\cdot}\mathbf{1}[e {\in} \mathcal{T}] {+} 0.4{\cdot}\mathbf{1}[|s| {\geq} 10] & y^* {=} \text{NA}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where A = Attributable, NA = Not Attributable.

where $\mathcal{T}$ is the six-category error taxonomy (Table~\ref{tab:error_taxonomy}).

\section{Adversarial Data Generation}
\label{app:adversarial}

The self-evolution loop (\S\ref{sec:evolution}) uses six targeted perturbation strategies to generate adversarial examples during the Probe stage.
Each strategy creates ``Not Attributable'' examples from ``Attributable'' (claim, source) pairs by applying controlled modifications.

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\small
\caption{Six adversarial perturbation strategies with examples. Each transforms an ``Attributable'' pair into a ``Not Attributable'' pair by modifying the claim.}
\label{tab:adversarial}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{p{2.4cm}p{4.2cm}p{4.6cm}p{2.0cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Original claim} & \textbf{Perturbed claim} & \textbf{Error type} \\
\midrule
Entity confusion & \textit{Apple} released the iPhone in 2007 & \textit{Samsung} released the iPhone in 2007 & entity\_sub. \\[3pt]
Numerical pert. & Revenue grew by \textit{15\%} in Q3 & Revenue grew by \textit{35\%} in Q3 & num.\_exag. \\[3pt]
Multi-hop graft & \textit{Born in London}, he studied at Oxford & Born in London, he studied at \textit{Cambridge} & fabrication \\[3pt]
Paraphrase & The CEO \textit{announced} the merger & The CEO \textit{denied} the merger & negation\_flip \\[3pt]
Presupposition & The temperature rose by 2\textdegree C & Temp.\ rose by 2\textdegree C, \textit{highest on record} & scope\_infl. \\[3pt]
Unansw.\ wrap & The study found positive results & Results were \textit{significant at $p{<}0.001$} & fabrication \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table*}

\paragraph{Quality filtering.}
Generated adversarial examples are filtered using three criteria:
(1)~the perturbation must be detectable by a human annotator (not trivially obvious);
(2)~the perturbed claim must remain grammatically fluent;
(3)~the perturbation must target the intended error type (validated by GPT-4o-mini cross-check).
Approximately 15\% of generated examples are discarded during filtering.
